The soup a war left behind

Dwaeji-gukbap wasn't invented in a restaurant kitchen. Here's where it actually came from — and how to order it without pointing helplessly at the menu.

The backstory

Winter, 1950. As the war pushed south, hundreds of thousands of refugees from Hamgyong Province were evacuated by sea from the port of Heungnam. Many landed in Busan with nothing. Near Sincheon Market, they started simmering pork bones — offcuts from nearby U.S. military bases — into a broth that could stretch a little meat into a lot of soup.

What they made barely resembles today's bowl, and for decades almost nobody admitted to liking it: pork itself was so stigmatized in the South that menus avoided naming it outright until the 1990s. What outlasted that stigma is now the dish locals order without a second thought.

Which bowl are you actually eating?

Miryang-style Clear broth. Widely credited as the original — even in Busan, well over 100 shops still put "Miryang" in their name.
Busan-style Cloudy, bone-simmered broth, cooked longer and harder. The version most visitors actually get served.
Daegu-style Built around organ meat as much as the broth — a rougher, more mineral bowl.

How to order like you've done this before

  • 따로국밥 (ttaro-gukbap) — the default: rice and soup arrive in separate bowls. Ask for "국밥" plain if you want them combined.
  • 순살 · 섞어 · 내장 — lean meat only, a mix, or organs. Say the word when you order; staff will assume "mixed" if you stay quiet.
  • 새우젓 (saeujeot) — salted shrimp on the side table. Add a little at a time; it's saltier than the spoon suggests.
  • 다대기 (dadegi) — the chili paste for heat. Stir it into the broth, not onto the rice.
  • 부추 (buchu) — chives on the side, tossed in dressing. Scatter over the top or eat as banchan — either is normal.
₩9,000+ is the going rate for a bowl in Busan now, up from around ₩7,000 just a few years ago. Same price at two shops can still mean two very different bowls — the broth is the tell, not the tag.
