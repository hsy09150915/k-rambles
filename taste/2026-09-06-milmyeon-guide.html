Milmyeon: a different story from naengmyeon

It's not just what Busan calls naengmyeon — it's a genuinely different dish. Here's how it ended up made from wheat flour, and where to actually try it today.

Last checked September 6, 2026 — restaurant details can change, so treat older information with caution.

Photo: shizu k (shezzz) at Flickr, CC BY-SA 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons

How it started — no ingredients left to make naengmyeon

The earliest solid documentation dates back to 1952. Jeong Han-geum, a woman who had run a naengmyeon restaurant (Dongchun Myeonok) in Hamhung, fled to Busan by U.S. military ship during the Korean War's 1.4 Retreat, and opened "Naeho Naengmyeon" in Uam-dong. At a time when buckwheat was impossible to get, mixing U.S.-aid wheat flour with sweet potato or potato starch to make something resembling naengmyeon is the explanation closest to accepted fact for how milmyeon began.

Using starch to offset wheat dough's tendency to turn soft and mushy ended up producing noodles far chewier than buckwheat noodles — and that's remained milmyeon's single biggest defining trait.

What "100 years of history" actually means

Some old-school shops advertise "100 years of history" or "4th generation since 1919." That's not counting milmyeon's own history — it's counting generations from when Jeong Han-geum's family ran a naengmyeon restaurant back in Hamhung. Milmyeon, the wheat-flour dish, only emerged in Busan after the Korean War, starting in the 1950s — that's the more accurate timeline.

How is it different from naengmyeon?

Because the noodles are wheat flour plus starch, they're far chewier and paler than buckwheat noodles. The broth also tends to be sweeter and tangier than Seoul-style naengmyeon — thought to reflect Gyeongsang-do tastes.

Bibim-milmyeon (right) Tossed in a sweet-and-spicy sauce. Some people add extra vinegar, but vinegar and mustard are traditionally condiments for mul-milmyeon (mul-naengmyeon). Toppings of egg, cucumber, and pear are similar to naengmyeon.

Don't take "Busan's top 3 milmyeon" at face value

Search for milmyeon restaurants and you'll keep running into the phrase "Busan's top 3 milmyeon" — but which three shops get named depends entirely on which source you check. Some list Gaya Milmyeon, Gaegeum Milmyeon, and Gukje Milmyeon; others list Gukje Milmyeon, Gaegeum Milmyeon, and Chunhachudong; still others list Choryang Milmyeon, Gaegeum Milmyeon, and Haeundae Gaya Milmyeon. Even Gaya Milmyeon (Gaegeum-dong) — frequently cited as the original — has since closed.

So rather than pinning down a fixed "top 3," below are the shops that keep coming up across multiple sources and are cross-checked as still open.

Good to know when ordering

  • Mul-milmyeon / Bibim-milmyeon — Both are usually on the menu. Even Koreans split roughly 50/50 between the two, so it comes down to personal taste. Bibim-milmyeon can be spicy, so pick based on your own spice tolerance.
  • Vinegar & mustard — Usually sitting on the table. Typically added to mul-milmyeon (mul-naengmyeon) to season it, and some people add extra vinegar to bibim-milmyeon too. Both have a strong flavor, so add a little at a time and taste as you go rather than pouring in a lot at once.
  • Extra noodles — Adding extra noodles is usually possible, and some places also let you add extra meat.
₩8,000~11,000 That's the going price range for a bowl of milmyeon in Busan these days. It varies by shop, and even at the same price, the broth style can be completely different.
