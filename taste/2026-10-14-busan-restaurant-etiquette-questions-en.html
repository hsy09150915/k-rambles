Busan Restaurant Guide for Foreigners: 12 Most-Asked Questions — Eating Alone, Self-Service, Paying and Tipping

If you've walked into a Korean restaurant not knowing where to sit, where to get water, or when to pay, this guide is for you. It covers the 12 questions foreigners ask most about eating out in Busan.

As of October 2026 — every restaurant does things a little differently. Check the signs at the entrance and on the table first.

At a glance

Korean restaurants differ from those back home in a few ways. The questions people ask most are whether you can eat alone, how you get water and side dishes, how you call staff, and where you pay. Here are the 12 questions foreigners ask most in Busan, in order.

1. Can I eat alone?

Many restaurants will seat a solo diner. However, some busy or tourist-heavy restaurants do not take solo diners at mealtimes. They need to sell as much as possible per table. In Korea the busiest times are usually 12:00–13:00 for lunch and 18:00–19:00 for dinner.

Restaurants that are not always full are usually fine with a solo diner, or so people say. If you avoid the rush, your chances go up. If you are turned away, it is the restaurant's situation, so just go to another place.

Also note that some restaurants require you to order at least two servings of certain dishes, so whether you can eat alone can depend on the menu. See section 3 below.

2. How do I find restaurants that welcome solo diners?

In the Naver Map app, search for "혼밥" (honbap) in Hangul Map. "Honbap" is Korean slang short for "혼자 먹는 밥" (honja meongneun bap), meaning "a meal eaten alone".

The search appears to work from reviews written by people who have already eaten there alone.

3. Which dishes are easy to eat alone?

It depends on the dish. Dishes sold in single portions are easy to order alone. Dishes meant for sharing often require an order of two servings or more. Take stews: kimchi jjigae (김치찌개) and doenjang jjigae (된장찌개) come as single portions at many restaurants, but kimchi jeongol (김치전골) is very likely to start at two servings.

This split is not absolute, and there are exceptions. Some places sell single-portion shabu-shabu or a single-serving meat set, and some places may require two servings even of gukbap or kalguksu.

Since it varies by restaurant, ask before you sit: "한 명이에요. 1인분 되나요?" (han myeong-ieyo. irinbun doenayo?), meaning "It's just me. Can I order one serving?" If you are curious about Busan's signature solo meal, see 20 Dwaeji Gukbap Spots, Picked by Busan Locals.

4. How do I get water, banchan, and cutlery?

You usually don't pay extra for more basic banchan (반찬, side dishes).

However, how you get water and banchan differs from place to place. The word to look for is "셀프" (selpeu). It comes from the English "self", and means you serve yourself.

5. How do I call staff?

Press the call bell (호출벨, hochulbel) on the table. It is usually at the edge or corner of the table, and at some restaurants it is attached to the outside of the drawer on the side of the table.

When you press it, your table number appears with a sound on a display where staff can easily see it. Staff see the number and come over quickly. If there is no bell, call out "여기요" (yeogiyo), meaning "Excuse me", or raise your hand and make eye contact.

6. How do I order?

Ordering works differently from place to place. There are three main ways.

MethodHow
With staffSit down, look at the menu, say "이거 주세요" (igeo juseyo), meaning "This one, please", and point at the dish
KioskChoose and pay at the screen near the entrance. Sit down after paying
Table orderOrder on the tablet on your table. Whether you pay now or later depends on the restaurant

At some restaurants, your order is only complete once you have paid on the table-order device. If a card reader sits next to the tablet, it is usually a pay-first system.

If a screen or menu is in Korean only, read it with the camera translation in the Papago app.

7. When and where do I pay?

At restaurants where you order from staff, pay at the counter near the entrance when you finish. Staff will not come to your table to take payment, even if you wait. Go to the counter and say "계산해 주세요" (gyesanhae juseyo), meaning "I'd like to pay, please".

Some high-end restaurants may bring the bill to your table and return your card there, but ordinary restaurants rarely do this.

Restaurants with kiosks or table-order devices often make you pay up front when you order (선결제, seon-gyeolje). The order screen will show whether it is pay-first.

8. Do I have to tip?

No. Korea has no tipping culture, so you don't need to tip. The price on the menu is what you pay.

Occasionally, if you leave money on the table without saying anything, a staff member will run after you to give it back, saying "you left your money".

9. How do I avoid arriving after closing time?

Many restaurants have a break time (브레이크타임, beureikeu taim) between lunch and dinner when they close. Some also have a last order (라스트오더, raseuteu odeo) that is earlier than closing time.

Check opening hours and days off in the restaurant's details in the Naver Map app.

10. Who cooks the meat at a Korean barbecue restaurant?

It depends on the restaurant. At some, staff cook it for you from start to finish, some cook only at the start, and at others you cook it yourself. When you sit down, ask "고기 구워 주시나요?" (gogi guwo jusinayo?), meaning "Do you grill the meat for us?"

Tongs and scissors are usually not on the table beforehand. Staff bring them along with the meat. Also remember that many places require at least two servings of grilled meat (see section 3) if you are alone.

In Korea, cutting food with scissors is normal. People from Western countries are often startled the first time, but these are scissors used only for food, so hygiene is not a concern. They are much faster and easier than a knife, which suits a culture that likes things quick and efficient.

11. Can I take leftovers home?

Whether you can take leftovers home depends on the restaurant. Many places will not pack soupy dishes. Ask before you eat: "포장돼요?" (pojangdwaeyo?), meaning "Can I get this to go?" If they say no, just leave the food and go.

12. How do I talk about allergies, vegetarian food, and spice?

In Korea, allergen labeling on menus is mandatory only for some restaurants, such as chains with 100 or more outlets that sell children's snack foods. Many neighborhood restaurants have no labels. So you need to tell staff directly when you order.

Menus often show the spice level with red chili pepper icons next to the photo of the dish. Usually it is one to three peppers. Three peppers can be very hot if you are not used to spicy food.

Korea's allergen list also includes buckwheat, pork, beef, and chicken, which differs from lists in other countries, so check it. If you are vegetarian, the broth in soups is often made from meat or anchovies, so ask "육수도 고기로 만들어요?" (yuksudo gogiro mandeureoyo?), meaning "Is the broth also made with meat?"

Vegan restaurants are not very common in Korea. A search for "비건" (vegan) in the Naver Map app returns few places, and many of them are dessert cafés. So choosing vegetable-based dishes at an ordinary restaurant can be faster. The dishes below usually do not contain meat.

Many stews, soups, and noodle dishes use meat or anchovy broth. Some banchan, such as myeolchi bokkeum (멸치볶음, stir-fried anchovies), eomuk (어묵, fish cake), and gyeran mari (계란말이, rolled omelet), are hard to identify by sight, so ask "이거 고기 들어가요?" (igeo gogi deureogayo?), meaning "Does this have meat (고기)?" Note that in Korea, "고기" (gogi) often makes people think only of beef or pork, not fish. If you don't eat fish either, ask separately: "이거 물고기 들어가요?" (igeo mulgogi deureogayo?), meaning "Does this have fish?"
