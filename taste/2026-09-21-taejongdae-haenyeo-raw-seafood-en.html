Taejongdae Haenyeo Village: Sashimi by the Sea, Still Sliced in a Basin?

It’s inside Busan’s most famous tourist spot, yet almost no foreign reviews mention it — we checked whether the scene from 30 years ago is still there.

As of September 2026

Taejongdae Haenyeochon — a mixed seafood plate

If you’ve been to Busan a few times over the years, you might remember it — at the foot of a cliff, ajummas with sea squirt, sea cucumber, and spoon worm (gaebul, 개불) in red rubber basins, slicing it right there on the spot.

Taejongdae is already on nearly every Busan itinerary. And yet, oddly enough, reviews from foreign visitors who actually bought and ate this seafood are almost impossible to find. Everyone sees the cliffs and the lighthouse, then walks right past what’s underneath.

"Is it still there?" you might be wondering. It is. Same as ever. Its official name is Taejongdae Haenyeochon (Taejongdae Haenyeo Village).

What exactly is on the menu

Roughly, here’s what’s on offer.

  • Sliced raw fish (saengseon-hoe) — closest thing here to Western-style sashimi, so it’ll probably feel the most familiar.
  • Horned turban shell (ppulsora) — a spiky sea snail. Chewy, salty.
  • Abalone (jeonbok) — considered an expensive, prized ingredient in Korea. Served thinly sliced raw, or cooked into porridge.
  • Sea cucumber (haesam) — a strange texture that’s slippery yet firm. The flavor itself is mild, so once you get used to the texture, it’s fine.
  • Sea squirt (meongge) — the most intensely oceanic of the bunch. More on this below.
  • Live octopus (sannakji) — cut into pieces and served with a sesame-oil-and-salt dip. It keeps squirming on the plate for a good while, which can be a real shock the first time you see it.
If you’ve never eaten raw seafood, this is a real challenge

If you’ve only ever eaten cooked seafood, this will honestly be a big challenge — squirming octopus, unfamiliar textures, and a strong ocean smell, all arriving at once.

But if you’re someone who enjoys pushing into something new, this can become one of the most memorable moments of your whole trip. You’re eating something that was alive moments ago, with the sound of waves right next to you.

It’s usually paired with Korean soju — exactly how Koreans themselves do it.

Koreans are so used to eating raw seafood that plenty of them genuinely love it. But even among Koreans, sea squirt is a real love-it-or-hate-it item — its intensely oceanic smell (closer to iodine) means quite a few people can’t stand it either. So if you try a bite of sea squirt and it’s not for you, don’t assume something’s wrong with your palate — Koreans are split right down the middle on this one too.

Who’s actually selling this

It’s not just the outfits — these are genuine haenyeo (traditional Korean women divers). According to a Korea Maritime and Ocean University survey, Yeongdo’s haenyeo dive at just two main spots, and this stretch of coast near the Taejongdae Jagalmadang (a jagalmadang is a pebble-yard stall area) is one of them.

Most of the vendors are older grandmothers, and one striking detail is that they prep the seafood wearing the same red rubber gloves you’ll see at any Korean restaurant or market. It’s such a common sight across Korea that locals barely notice it — but for a foreign visitor, it’s likely to stand out as something fresh and new.

Taejongdae: down at the foot of the cliff

Walk down the stairs from the observatory toward the lighthouse, and you’ll find open-air stalls run by haenyeo grandmothers. Pick what you want, they slice it on the spot, and you sit in a plastic tent right beside it, eating with a view of the sea.

One Western travel blog review we found describes it as — "inside a warm tent, with the blue sea right there, seeing a live fish for the first time." A lot of the tone leaned toward novelty and romance.

Based on visitor accounts, a plate of turban shell and octopus ran about KRW 50,000 (this can vary with the season and catch).

How to think about the price

To a Korean who has plenty of seafood markets and raw-fish restaurants to compare against, this isn’t especially good value — there are cheaper places around Busan serving similar things.

But if the ingredients themselves are unfamiliar and your sense of what things "should" cost is calibrated elsewhere, it reads differently. It’s probably more accurate to think of the price as covering not just "how much is this food," but the seat and the experience of eating it with the sea right at the foot of a cliff.

Gamjihaebyeon-gil Jagalmadang: if you’d rather it be cooked

Near the entrance to Taejongdae Resort Park, at 81 Gamjihaebyeon-gil, there’s a second jagalmadang. This one is mostly cooked food rather than raw — scallop and fan mussel grills, grilled abalone, grilled eel, grilled shrimp, and seafood ramyeon are all on offer. If raw seafood feels like too much, this is the easier option.

Rows of tented stalls line the area, and there’s a small beach right beside it. Like Huinnyeoul Culture Village, it looks out over Busan’s western sea, which makes it especially nice around sunset.

In fact, thanks to its less-intimidating grilled-clam menu, this spot gets mentioned more often in Western travel blogs and reviews than the raw-seafood haenyeo stalls by the lighthouse — it has reviews on Tripadvisor, and the travel platform NOL even has its own dedicated page for it.

Good to know before you go

  • According to Taejongdae Resort Park’s own guide, there are three "jagalmadang" open-air stall areas inside the park in total — some sell raw seafood, some grilled shellfish, so it’s worth asking ahead which one you’re headed to.
  • You can ride the Danubi Train (the park’s circular shuttle) from the entrance to the lighthouse, but the stairs down from the lighthouse to the shore have to be walked.
  • Card payment often doesn’t work, since these are street stalls — a lot of them have no card terminal at all. Bring some cash.
  • Some stay open into the evening or even late at night, but it varies by stall, so visiting before sunset is the safer bet.

How to get there

  • From Busan Station — bus 101 from the stop in front of the station, about KRW 1,600, roughly 40 minutes
  • From Nampo Station — bus 8 or 30 from the stop next to Exit 6, about KRW 1,600, roughly 35 minutes

Both routes take you to the entrance of Taejongdae Resort Park — from there, the Danubi Train or a walk gets you to the lighthouse and the jagalmadang.

Prices and hours can shift with the season and catch. Worth double-checking before you visit.
