Busan Pork Meals Guide: Ppyeo-haejangguk, Jeyuk-bokkeum, Dwaeji-bulbaek and Kimchi-jjigae

This guide covers pork dishes that are not grilled. It explains how ppyeo-haejangguk (뼈해장국), jeyuk-bokkeum (제육볶음), dwaeji-bulbaek (돼지불백) and kimchi-jjigae (김치찌개) differ, from menu names to restaurants.

As of October 28, 2026 — Opening days and menus may change. Check before you go.

0. Good to Know First - Guk (국), Tang (탕), Jjigae (찌개) and Bokkeum (볶음)

This guide collects pork dishes that are not grilled. For grilled pork such as samgyeopsal (삼겹살), moksal (목살) and galbi (갈비), see the Busan Pork BBQ Guide, and for dwaeji-gukbap (돼지국밥, pork and rice soup), see the Busan Dwaeji Gukbap Guide.

Many names on a menu look alike. The Standard Korean Language Dictionary (표준국어대사전) explains them like this.

NameMeaning
Guk (국), tang (탕)Guk is a dish made by pouring plenty of water over meat, fish or vegetables, seasoning it and boiling it. Tang is the honorific word for guk. It can also mean a guk simmered for a long time until the broth turns rich
Jjigae (찌개)A dish made by putting meat, vegetables, tofu and the like into an earthenware pot or small pot with a thick, small amount of broth, seasoning it with soy sauce, soybean paste or chili paste, and boiling it. It has less broth than guk
Bokkeum (볶음)A dish of ingredients that are seasoned and stir-fried. It has almost no broth

However, these names are not separated clearly by the amount or richness of the broth. The National Institute of Korean Language (국립국어원) also explains that guk, tang, jjigae and jeongol (전골) are not words with clearly different meanings but are divided by custom.

That is why each food has a name that is almost fixed. Kimchi is called jjigae (kimchi-jjigae, 김치찌개), seaweed is called guk (miyeok-guk, 미역국) and seafood is called tang (haemul-tang, 해물탕). People do not usually say miyeok-tang or kimchi-tang. Gamjatang (감자탕) has tang in its name, but it is closer to a jjigae, served in a pot where you pick out the ingredients.

How a dish is eaten with rice also differs by menu. The Korean Food Culture Dictionary (한식문화사전) explains that guk is a basic dish served with rice. One-bowl dishes eaten with rice, such as haejangguk (해장국), sundae-gukbap (순댓국) and yukgaejang (육개장), come with rice when you order. Dishes that were originally drinking snacks, such as gamjatang or jeongol, need rice ordered separately.

So trying to tell the names apart by meaning alone often does not work. After eating Korean food a few times, the name that goes with each menu becomes familiar. It is easier to remember each menu name as one word than to work out what the name means.

Haejangguk (해장국) is a soup eaten to settle the stomach after a night of drinking. Many people also eat it as a hearty breakfast or lunch without drinking.

1. Ppyeo-haejangguk (뼈해장국) and Gamjatang (감자탕) - Spicy soups made from pork back-bones

Ppyeo-haejangguk (뼈해장국) is a haejangguk made with broth from pork or beef bones.Ppyeodagwi-haejangguk (뼈다귀해장국) is another name for it. You pick the meat off the bones and mix rice into the broth.

Ppyeo-haejangguk has plenty of broth, but unlike an ordinary guk eaten with rice, the seasoning is strong and spicy. It sometimes comes in an earthenware pot like a jjigae.

By looks alone, it would not be strange to call it a guk, tang or jjigae. It is a one-bowl dish eaten with rice, so the name haejangguk (guk) became fixed.

Photo: Mannagamjatang Daeyeon Branch, 291-1 Daeyeon-dong, Nam-gu, Busan (맛나감자탕 대연점, 부산 남구 대연동 291-1)

Gamjatang (감자탕) is a jjigae made by boiling pork back-bones with seasonings such as potato, perilla seeds, scallions and garlic. The Korea Tourism Organization (한국관광공사) introduces it in English as Pork Back-bone Stew.

Photo: Mannagamjatang Daeyeon Branch, 291-1 Daeyeon-dong, Nam-gu, Busan (맛나감자탕 대연점, 부산 남구 대연동 291-1)

The gamja (감자) in the name does not mean the vegetable potato. There is no settled origin. One theory says it comes from a part of the pork back-bone called the gamja-bone (감자뼈), and another says the spinal cord was called gamja. Some restaurants do put potatoes in, but that is not why the name was given.

Many restaurants sell both dishes. Usually ppyeo-haejangguk comes as a single-serving earthenware pot, and gamjatang comes in a pot shared by several people. At most of the restaurants below you can eat ppyeo-haejangguk alone.

Many gamjatang restaurants need rice ordered separately. Many places serve ppyeo-haejangguk with rice, but it is safer to ask once when you order.

2. Jeyuk-bokkeum (제육볶음) and Duruchigi (두루치기) - Spicy stir-fried pork

Jeyuk-bokkeum (제육볶음) is pork that is seasoned and stir-fried with vegetables such as garlic chives and onion. Jeyuk (제육) means pork. The Korea Tourism Organization introduces it as Spicy Stir-fried Pork and says it is popular as a side dish with rice.

Duruchigi (두루치기) is made by stir-frying meat and vegetables and then adding a little seasoned broth and simmering. The big difference is that jeyuk-bokkeum has almost no broth, while duruchigi has some. The amount of broth varies by restaurant, so some serve a fair amount and others serve very little.

Photo: Gongwon Galbi, 86-14 Yongho-dong, Nam-gu, Busan (공원갈비, 부산 남구 용호동 86-14)

Restaurants also mix the names, so if you want one form, ask whether it comes with broth. When you order, it is usually served as a jeongsik (정식, set meal) with rice and side dishes.

You get far more chances to eat jeyuk-bokkeum or duruchigi at restaurants that sell it as an extra beside their main dishes than at specialty restaurants that sell only this.

3. Dwaeji-bulbaek (돼지불백) - A pork bulgogi set meal in soy sauce seasoning

Dwaeji-bulbaek (돼지불백) is a meal of white rice and pork bulgogi with a few side dishes. The Urimalsaem (우리말샘) dictionary explains it that way. It is short for bulgogi-baekban (불고기 백반), and menus write it differently by restaurant: bulbaek (불백), dwaeji-bulbaek, bulgogi-baekban, dwaeji-bulgogi (돼지불고기) and jeyuk-bulgogi (제육불고기). Even with different names, a set of pork bulgogi served with rice and side dishes is the same kind of meal.

Jeyuk-bokkeum is a single meat dish, while dwaeji-bulbaek is a whole table with rice and side dishes. Bulbaek usually comes with a soup or jjigae.

Photo: Choryang Bulbaek, 667-8 Choryang-dong, Dong-gu, Busan (초량불백, 부산 동구 초량동 667-8)

Choryang Bulbaek Street (초량 불백 거리)

In Choryang, Dong-gu, Busan, there is an alley where bulbaek restaurants are clustered. Somunnan Bulbaek (소문난불백) has a yellow sign and Choryang Bulbaek (초량불백) has an orange sign. The two share the same road address, so they can be confusing when you search in a map app. Go by the sign color. There are more restaurants with similar names nearby.

4. Pork Kimchi-jjigae (김치찌개), Kimchi-jjim (김치찜) and Dwaeji-jjigae (돼지찌개) - Dishes boiled and steamed with kimchi

Kimchi-jjigae (김치찌개) is a jjigae boiled with kimchi. The Korean Food Culture Dictionary explains the modern kimchi-jjigae as a dish made by boiling well-fermented napa cabbage kimchi with pork, tofu and the like. The pork is usually cut small. It goes well as a meal or as a drinking snack.

Many restaurants write it as dwaeji-kimchi-jjigae (돼지김치찌개). When you order, rice and side dishes come with it.

Photo: Gorang Sikdang, 402-34 Deokcheon-dong, Buk-gu, Busan (고랑식당, 부산 북구 덕천동 402-34)

Kimchi-jjim (김치찜) is a dish of fermented kimchi with meat, seasoned and steamed. Wikipedia explains it this way. Health Chosun (헬스조선) says that with the same ingredients, adding a lot of water makes kimchi-guk (김칫국), adding less makes kimchi-jjigae, and turning down the heat and simmering it down makes kimchi-jjim.

Photo: Lee Hak-su Hanokjip Kimchi-jjim Main Branch, 38-4 Daeyeon-dong, Nam-gu, Busan (이학수 한옥집김치찜 본점, 부산 남구 대연동 38-4)

So kimchi-jjigae and kimchi-jjim are like brothers that differ in the amount of broth. The flavor base is similar, so they are grouped in one table.

However, many restaurants take orders for kimchi-jjim from 2 portions upward. If you are alone, ask first whether there is a single-serving menu.

Dwaeji-jjigae (돼지찌개) has a similar name but differs by restaurant. Some boil it with kimchi, much like kimchi-jjigae, and others simmer it spicy with gochujang or chili powder and no kimchi. The tables below separate restaurants that use kimchi from those that do not.

5. Reading the Menu - Terms for Soups and Stir-fries

TermMeaning
Ppyeo-haejangguk (뼈해장국), ppyeodagwi-haejangguk (뼈다귀해장국)A haejangguk boiled in broth made from pork bones
Gamjatang (감자탕)A jjigae boiled with potato, perilla seeds and seasonings on pork back-bones. It is sold in small, medium and large sizes
Ugeoji (우거지)The outer leaves of napa cabbage or radish, boiled and dried. Ugeoji-haejangguk is a haejangguk with plenty of ugeoji
Jeyuk (제육)Means pork
Jeongsik (정식), baekban (백반)A menu item that serves rice, soup and side dishes as one table. Even with the same makeup, restaurants write it differently, such as bulgogi-jeongsik (불고기정식) or bulgogi-baekban (불고기백반)
Kimchi-jjim (김치찜)A dish of fermented kimchi with meat, seasoned and steamed. It has less broth than kimchi-jjigae
Gonggibap (공기밥)A bowl of rice. Some restaurants charge for it separately
Sari (사리)Extra noodles, such as ramyeon or dangmyeon (당면, glass noodles), added to a soup dish
So (소), jung (중), dae (대)Pot sizes (small, medium, large). How many people each serves differs by restaurant, so ask when you order
