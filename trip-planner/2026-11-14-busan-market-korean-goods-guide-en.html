What to Buy at Busan's Traditional Markets: Korean Blankets to Hand-Forged Knives

Watch a few Korean dramas and you keep seeing the same things: a flower-print mink blanket, a ttukbaegi pot bubbling on the table, a scrub mitt at the jjimjilbang (Korean bathhouse), a grandma in a floral vest. You will find these far more easily at traditional markets than at department stores.

This guide goes item by item and tells you where in Busan to buy them.

As of November 24, 2026 — opening hours and closing days vary by shop. Check a map app again before you go.

For each item we also note which travelers buy it most and why. This comes from news reports and travelers' own reviews.

Busanjin Market and Bujeon Market are both on subway Line 1, so they are easy to visit on the same day. A sample route is in section 9.

1. Blankets and bedding — Busanjin Market, basement 1

Korean bedding has become something travelers make time to buy. At Seoul's Gwangjang Market, it is known among Taiwanese and Singaporean visitors as a "must-do bedding stop."

Why do travelers love it?

Shoppers from Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore and Thailand stand out. In a June 2026 Korea Herald article, a bedding store manager said Taiwanese customers come most, followed by Hong Kong, Singapore and Thailand. At one bedding shop in Gwangjang Market, about 70% of customers are foreigners.

Trust in "made in Korea" matters too. In the same article, a manager said some Chinese-speaking customers first ask whether an item is made in China, because they want bedding made in Korea.

The most popular are light, silky modal quilts and cooling summer blankets. Korea has four distinct seasons, so there are bedding types for every season, and you can pick a summer blanket to use back home in a hot country.

In Busan, the place to shop for bedding is Busanjin Market (부산진시장). Koreans go there to buy bedding, tableware and hanbok when preparing for a wedding, so many bedding shops are gathered on basement level 1.

What is a "mink blanket"?

It is not real mink. The name means "as soft as mink." In Korea, thick microfiber or faux-sheepskin blankets are usually called mink blankets (밍크담요, mingkeu damyo).

Blankets with big flowers or a tiger picture are a classic sight in grandma's house in Korean dramas.

Busanjin Market (부산진시장)

Address — 24 Jinsijang-ro, Dong-gu, Busan (Beomil-dong)

Getting there — About 5 minutes on foot from Exit 1 of Beomil Station, subway Line 1

Hours — Monday to Saturday. Shops open around 7 a.m., and many close after 6 p.m.

2. Ttukbaegi, brassware and spoon sets — market kitchenware shops

You don't need to hunt for ttukbaegi, brassware and spoon sets separately. Most kitchenware shops in Korean traditional markets sell all three.

In Busan, kitchenware shops are gathered on basement level 1 of Busanjin Market. Shops such as Chonggak Geureut (총각그릇), Hyundai Geureut (현대그릇) and Samyoung Geureut (삼영그릇) sell wedding tableware sets along with ttukbaegi, spoon sets and pots. You can look around while you are there for bedding.

What are they, and why do travelers love them?

Ttukbaegi (뚝배기) is a thick earthenware pot. You boil a stew on the stove in it and serve it in the same pot. Like a cast-iron pot, it holds heat for a long time, so travelers who cook Korean food at home love it. Some overseas buyers write that they fell for it in Korea and wished they had brought more home. Don't wash it with dish soap. Earthenware has many tiny pores you can't see, and soap that soaks into them is hard to rinse out and can seep into your next stew. Soak it in water and scrub it with a brush or sponge.

Brassware (놋그릇, notgeureut, also called yugi) is golden tableware made from copper and tin. It was once used for honored guests and ancestral rites, and in 1983 the craft was designated a National Intangible Cultural Heritage. It is heavy and needs care, so overseas travel guides recommend it "only if you will really use it at home."

Sujeo (수저) is a set of a spoon and chopsticks. Korean chopsticks are flat and made of metal, so they feel different in your hand if you are used to wooden ones. They are light and hard to break, which makes them one of the easiest Korean souvenirs.

If you are staying in Haeundae, Samyoung Jubang (삼영주방) at the entrance of Haeundae Traditional Market is convenient. It is a second-generation kitchenware shop open since 1987, with brassware, ttukbaegi and Korean-made spoon sets inside. More and more foreign travelers stop by.

3. Scrub mitts and socks — around Bupyeong Kkangtong Market

The green or red striped scrub mitt (Italy towel, 이태리타월) has become a popular Korean souvenir among foreign travelers.

Why do travelers love it?

The biggest reason is the shock of the first scrub. Reviews from abroad are full of lines like "I shower every day with soap, and I was shocked at how much dead skin came off my clean body" and "You don't know how much dead skin you have until you use this."

After that, many say a shower without it doesn't feel clean. A writer for the US outlet The Root wrote, after a body scrub in New York's Koreatown, "I thought I knew how to bathe," and that you are not really clean until you have had one. One Western blogger thought they had dry skin, then realized they had never properly exfoliated.

Smoother skin is another reason. Many reviews say it helped with small bumps on arms and legs, ingrown hairs and back acne, and some people now say they can't go without it.

A reporter for a Malaysian outlet tried it after seeing it on TikTok and wrote that it was "life-changing" and she "felt like a clean newborn." Because she had heard there were fakes, she made a point of ordering one made in Korea.

Travelers from Japan, China and the West all buy them. Sellers at Seoul's Namdaemun Market say even travelers from North America and Europe, where public bathhouse culture is unfamiliar, buy them.

As jjimjilbang visits and body scrubs (seshin, 세신) have become part of travel plans, people buy scrub mitts to take home too. According to a 2026 report, half the customers at Spa Land Centum City in Busan are foreigners, and foreigners who try a body scrub are surprised at how their skin feels afterward.

They are a hit on social media too. In 2026, reports said bath goods from Seoul's Namdaemun Market, such as scrub mitts and scrub soap, spread on social media and drew young Koreans and then foreign shoppers.

Cheap and small, they make perfect souvenirs. A traveler who bought several at Namdaemun Market said they are "cheap and don't take up much space, so they're good souvenirs."

The Italy towel was born in Busan

In 1967, Hanil Textile, a textile factory in Choeup-dong, Busan, imported viscose rayon fabric from Italy. The surface turned out to be too rough to use for clothes or towels.

Someone tried scrubbing with the fabric that was nearly thrown away, and it worked well. It became a scrub towel, and because the fabric came from Italy it was named the Italy towel. Before that, people scrubbed with a rolled-up towel or a stone wrapped in cloth.

It spread nationwide thanks to public bathhouses. As bathhouses multiplied after the Korean War, scrubbing with this towel became part of Korean bathing culture. From the 1960s, the number of seshinsa (세신사), attendants who scrub customers, also grew sharply.

When the utility model right expired in 1976, many companies started making it. It became so common that almost every Korean has used one. In 2017, it came 6th in a Facebook vote held by the Korean Intellectual Property Office, "Top 10 Inventions That Made Korea Shine."

Accounts differ on who first made it, but they all agree it was born in Busan.

In Busan, Bupyeong Kkangtong Market (부평깡통시장) is the well-known place for bath goods. In 2026, more shoppers came after seeing it on social media, and it is now called a "bath-goods mecca." Shops sell scrub mitts in different colors and roughness, scrub soap, sauna hats and shower puffs.

Choosing a scrub mitt

Roughness differs by color, but each company uses its own scale, so check the strength label on the package. If it is your first time, go for a softer one.

Kkangtong Market also has a night market in the evening. Buy bath goods during the day, then come back for night-market food.

Korean socks are another good buy in the same neighborhood. Kkangtong Market and the alleys of Nampo-dong have many sock shops and stalls.

Why do travelers love it?

Cheap and light, so it is easy to buy several pairs at once. Bundle deals, where you get a free pair when you buy a few, are common. Travel writers call Korean socks "the souvenir nobody plans to buy, but everybody does."

The designs are fun. From K-pop idols, characters and traditional patterns to ramen packets and the face on a banknote, the person you give them to will know right away they came from Korea.

Many say the quality is good for the price.

Filipino travelers post TikTok videos of buying Korean socks as pasalubong, gifts to hand out back home.

Some reviews mention buying toe socks along with bath goods at Kkangtong Market.

4. Grandma vests and momppae pants — Bujeon Market

A grandma vest is a quilted vest with a floral print. Because grandmothers wear it while making kimchi for winter (kimjang), it is also called a kimjang vest (김장조끼).

Momppae pants (몸빼바지) are loose floral pants with an elastic waist. Market vendors and grandmothers who farm wear them a lot, so they are known as the outfit that says "Korean countryside."

Why do travelers love it?

In winter 2025, Jennie of BLACKPINK and Karina of aespa posted photos of themselves in kimjang vests, and the vest became famous abroad as the "Korean grandma vest." You can see the two of them wearing it in this Korea Times article.

TikTok and Instagram filled up with reactions like "so tacky but cute" and "why does this look good?" It also fit the "halmaenial" trend (from halmeoni, Korean for grandma, plus millennial), young people enjoying grandma tastes, a Korean cousin of the "grandmacore" trend.

In early 2026, a news report showed a Singaporean traveler at Seoul's Namdaemun Market picking out a vest and saying, "That's the one Jennie wore!" and "I saw it everywhere on Instagram Reels and TikTok."

The luxury vest that looked familiar

In early 2026, a post titled "Valentino copying a Korean hot item" went viral on an overseas online community. It pointed out that a floral vest from the Italian luxury brand Valentino looked like a kimjang vest.

People said a floral down vest from Moncler, a brand founded in France, looked similar too.

Korean netizens joked, "The kimjang vest is touring Europe" and "Our grandmas were ahead of fashion." A vest from the market ended up talked about alongside world fashion houses.

In Busan, the place to find these clothes is Bujeon Market (부전시장). It is one of the largest traditional markets in Busan, and clothing shops in the market alleys and the underground shopping arcade at Bujeon Station sell vests and momppae pants.

5. Hanbok, mother-of-pearl boxes and bojagi — back at Busanjin Market

Busanjin Market is also a hanbok market. At the hanbok section in the basement and the fabric shops on the first floor, you can order or buy hanbok, and they also sell baby hanbok and bokjumeoni (lucky pouches).

Some shops prepare wedding gifts. Seongil (성일) on basement level 1 is known for beautifully wrapping wedding gifts. It sells mother-of-pearl boxes and wraps presents in bojagi.

Why do travelers love it?

Jewelry boxes and mirrors made with mother-of-pearl (jagae, 자개) are popular with both Koreans and foreigners at Seoul's Namdaemun Market as well. The rainbow shimmer that changes with the light catches the eye.

What are mother-of-pearl and bojagi?

Mother-of-pearl (자개, jagae) is a craft of cutting the rainbow-colored inner layer of abalone or other shells into thin pieces and inlaying them. The mother-of-pearl wardrobe in grandma's house is a classic.

Bojagi (보자기) is a traditional wrapping cloth. Tying a gift in bojagi instead of putting it in a box is the Korean way to wrap a present.

A bojagi looks completely different depending on how you tie the knot. On top of the basic ways, wrapping the cloth around a box or tying the four corners together, you finish by folding the knot into a ribbon, a rose or a fan.

There are so many knots that some bojagi studios teach more than 20. A tassel ornament on top of the knot makes it even prettier.

Bojagi wrapping classes for foreign travelers are held in many places. A bojagi you tied yourself makes a Korean souvenir on its own.

6. Forged knives, homi and sickles — Gaya Forge, Gimhae

A daejanggan (대장간) is a forge where iron is heated and hammered into knives and farm tools. Forges often appear in Korean historical dramas, but only a few remain in the country today.

The easiest one to visit near Busan is Gaya Forge (가야대장간) in Gimhae. It is a second-generation forge that makes and sells kitchen knives, homi, sickles and cast-iron pots.

Why do travelers love it?

In 2026, on the MBC every1 show "Welcome, First Time in Korea?", a group of Italian Michelin-star chefs stopped by. After trying the knives, they bought 47 of them, and one chef bought 26 by himself as gifts for his colleagues.

Keep an eye out for the homi (호미) too. The Korean hand hoe, bent at a right angle, is known abroad as the "Korean hand plow," and in 2018 it made the top 10 in Amazon's gardening tool sales. You can dig, weed and plant seedlings with one tool, so gardeners love it.

Photo: MBC every1 YouTube

Photo: Gaya Forge (67-10 Bonghwangdae-gil, Gimhae-si, Gyeongnam)

You may not get a knife right away

Orders have piled up since the show aired. If the knife you want is not in the shop, you may need to order it and wait more than four months.

Homi, sickles and small items are often available to take home on the spot. If you really want a knife, call ahead to ask about stock.

While you're in Gimhae

The Bonghwang-dong area around Gaya Forge is Bongnidan-gil (봉리단길), a street full of cafes. The Tomb of King Suro and Gimhae National Museum are close by, so it makes an easy half-day trip.

Gimhae was home to the ancient Gaya kingdom, famous for its iron. See Gaya iron artifacts at Gimhae National Museum, then visit the forge, and the story connects.

In Busan itself, two old forges, Deokchang Danjo (덕창단조) and Daewon Danjo (대원단조), remain at Jobangap (조방앞) in Beomil-dong. They mainly make construction tools and farm tools to order, so they are more for a look as you pass by than for souvenir shopping. They are within walking distance of Busanjin Market.

7. Museum goods — Busan Museum shop

Museum goods have become popular Korean travel souvenirs. MU:DS, the goods brand of the National Museum of Korea in Seoul, made headlines with its Pensive Bodhisattva miniatures and magpie-and-tiger badges.

Busan Museum also makes goods based on its own collection. A highlight is a tiger painting from the late Joseon period. It went to Japan through Choryang Waegwan, the Japanese trading post in Busan, and was popular in Japan, where there were no wild tigers.

Why do travelers love it?

After the 2025 Netflix animated film "KPop Demon Hunters" became a global hit, about 90,000 magpie and tiger badges, which look like the tiger and magpie in the film, were sold in 2025 alone. They sold out again and again as soon as new stock came in.

The magpie and tiger (jakhodo) is a favorite subject of Korean folk painting. The tiger wards off bad luck, and the magpie brings good news. Cute and full of good wishes, it makes a great gift.

In 2025, about 10% of MU:DS buyers at the National Museum of Korea were foreigners.

Busan Museum also made goods from the tiger painting in its collection to ride the tiger boom.

ItemHow to take it home
Knives, sicklesNot allowed in your carry-on. Always put them in your checked baggage.
Blankets, beddingBulky. Ask the shop to vacuum-pack them, or send them by international parcel from the post office.
TtukbaegiBreaks easily. Wrap it in clothes and put it in your checked baggage.
BrasswareHeavy. Check your baggage weight limit first.
