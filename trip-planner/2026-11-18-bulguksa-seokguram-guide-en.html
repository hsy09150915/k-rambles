Bulguksa Temple and Seokguram Grotto in Gyeongju: Half-Day Route and Must-Sees

Bulguksa Temple (불국사) and Seokguram Grotto (석굴암) are Silla-era sites on the slopes of Tohamsan Mountain (토함산) in Gyeongju. They became a UNESCO World Heritage Site together in 1995.

The two are about 20 minutes apart by bus, or about 50 minutes on foot along a forest trail, so many people see both in half a day. This guide covers the order to visit, the highlights that are easy to miss, and the bus and trail up to Seokguram.

1. What are they?

Bulguksa is a temple that the chief minister Kim Dae-seong began building in 751, under King Gyeongdeok of Silla, and that was completed in 774. Kim Dae-seong began Seokguram around the same time.

The Samguk Yusa (삼국유사), a history book from the Goryeo period, tells the story that Kim Dae-seong built Bulguksa for his parents in this life and Seokguram for his parents in a previous life.

Bulguksa means "Temple of the Buddha Land." It was built to bring the Buddha's world that the Silla people dreamed of down to earth, so even a single staircase or pagoda carries Buddhist meaning.

In December 1995, the two were listed together as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

2. What order should you see them in?

Suggested order (by bus)

BulguksaBus 12SeokguramBus 12 or forest trailBulguksa

If you're going by bus, see Bulguksa first, then take bus 12 up to Seokguram and come back down. All buses from the city center stop at Bulguksa, and only bus 12 goes on to Seokguram.

Bulguksa takes about 1 hour 30 minutes to look around, and Seokguram about 1 hour. Add the time spent waiting for and riding bus 12, and the whole visit comes to about 4 hours.

Arrive at Bulguksa by 2 p.m. at the latest

Bus 12 runs about once an hour, and in winter the last entry is at 5 p.m. If you go late in the afternoon, you'll run into Seokguram's last entry and the last bus, and end up seeing only one of the two.

If you reach Bulguksa at 9 a.m., you can see both by around lunchtime and spend the afternoon walking around the city center. The downtown walking route is in Gyeongju Downtown Walking Route.

3. What to see at Bulguksa

From the front, Bulguksa looks like a row of buildings sitting on top of a high stone terrace. The stone terrace, the staircases and the two stone pagodas are original, from 8th-century Silla.

All the wooden buildings were burned in 1593, during the Imjin War (Japan's invasion of Joseon Korea), because the temple had served as a base for Korean volunteer fighters. Most of the buildings you see today date from a major restoration from 1969 to 1973.

Cheongungyo and Baegungyo - the bridges into the Buddha Land

This is the large stone staircase you see first after the main gate. The lower flight is Baegungyo (백운교) and the upper flight is Cheongungyo (청운교), and together they are a National Treasure.

They are called "bridges" even though they are stairs, because they stand for crossing over from the human world into the world of the Buddha. The semicircular stone arch under the stairs is called a hongye (홍예), meaning a "rainbow arch." It shows an early form of the stone arches later used in Korean bridges and fortress gates.

You can no longer climb these stairs, so you go around to the side to get in.

To the left is a smaller stone staircase leading up to Geungnakjeon Hall: Yeonhwagyo (연화교) and Chilbogyo (칠보교). It is also a National Treasure, and the gate at the top is Anyangmun (안양문).

Dabotap and Seokgatap - two very different pagodas

In the courtyard in front of Daeungjeon, the main hall, two pagodas of completely different shapes face each other. Both are National Treasures.

  • Dabotap (다보탑, east): An ornate pagoda with stairs, railings and even a stone lion. It is 10.29 m tall. This is the pagoda shown on Korea's 10-won coin.
  • Seokgatap (석가탑, west): A plain three-story pagoda with no decoration. At 10.75 m tall, it is regarded as the classic form of the Unified Silla stone pagoda.

The two pagodas stand side by side to retell a story from the Lotus Sutra. When the Buddha Shakyamuni was preaching, Prabhutaratna (Dabo in Korean), a Buddha of the past, appeared inside a pagoda and confirmed that his words were true.

In 1966, while Seokgatap was being repaired, a scroll of Buddhist scripture called the Mugujeonggwang Great Dharani Sutra (무구정광대다라니경) was found inside it. Printed in 8th-century Silla, it is regarded as the oldest woodblock print in the world.

Gilt-bronze Buddhas and the Bulguksa Museum

Geungnakjeon Hall holds a seated gilt-bronze Amitabha Buddha, and Birojeon Hall a seated gilt-bronze Vairocana Buddha. Both were made in the 8th century and are National Treasures.

At the Bulguksa Museum inside the temple grounds, you can see the original reliquary items found in Seokgatap. Unlike the temple, the museum charges its own admission and is closed on Mondays.

4. What to see at Seokguram

Seokguram is not a cave dug out of rock. It is a man-made grotto built from granite blocks, each one cut and stacked by hand. The domed ceiling was assembled from stone and then covered with earth.

Cave temples in India and China used natural caves or were carved out of rock. Grottoes built by stacking stone are hard to find, so Seokguram is regarded as a Silla masterpiece that brings together architecture, mathematics, religion and art.

In the center of the grotto sits the main Buddha, about 3.4 m tall, facing toward the East Sea. Bodhisattvas, disciples and guardian deities of the Buddhist law are carved on the surrounding walls.

You can only see the main Buddha through a glass wall

You can't go inside the grotto; you view the main Buddha from outside a glass wall at the entrance and leave. Photos are not allowed inside either. So the time you actually spend looking at the statue is just over 10 minutes.

There is a reason for this. Korea was under Japanese colonial rule from 1910 to 1945, and from 1913 to 1915 the Japanese Government-General of Korea took Seokguram apart, rebuilt it and covered the outside with concrete.

After that, damp and moss formed inside the grotto, and the problems continued even after repairs in the 1960s. In the end, machines have controlled the humidity and temperature since 1966, and since 1976 visitors have only been able to look from outside the glass wall.

From the parking lot to Seokguram is about a 10-minute walk on a flat path. When heavy rain, typhoon or heavy snow advisories or warnings are issued, access to Seokguram may be closed.

Take bus 12 from the stop in front of Bulguksa and get off at the Seokguram parking lot. With one bus an hour, missing it means a long wait. When you reach Bulguksa, check the bus 12 departure time first.

See the bus 12 route

The forest trail is managed by the Korea National Park Service. It runs 2.2 km from Bulguksa to the Seokguram parking lot and is lovely with cherry blossoms in spring and red leaves in autumn.

However, the stone-paved path and steps go on and on, so it's hard with a stroller or wheelchair, and some stretches have a risk of falling rocks. Many people take the bus up and walk the trail down.

The trail is sometimes closed for construction or during the wildfire prevention period. Even during the autumn 2026 wildfire prevention period (November 15 to December 15), the 2.2 km section from Bulguksa to the Seokguram parking lot stays open. The path from the Seokguram parking lot to the top of Tohamsan Mountain is closed for construction.

6. Before you go

  • Arrival time: Arrive at Bulguksa by 2 p.m. at the latest. In winter the last entry is at 5 p.m.
  • Bus 12: It runs about once an hour. Check the departure time from Bulguksa and the time of the bus back down from Seokguram first.
  • Seokguram: You view it through a glass wall, and you can't take photos inside.
  • Admission: Both sites are free. Only the Bulguksa Museum charges its own admission.
  • Shoes: Bulguksa has a lot of stairs, and if you plan to take the forest trail, comfortable sneakers are a good idea.
