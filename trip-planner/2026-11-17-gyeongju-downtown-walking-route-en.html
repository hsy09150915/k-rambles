Gyeongju Downtown Walking Route: Hwangnidan-gil, Daereungwon and Cheomseongdae to Donggung Palace at Night

The Silla-era sites in downtown Gyeongju sit within a 2 km radius, so you can see them on foot in a day, no bus needed. This route starts at Hwangnidan-gil, passes Daereungwon, Cheomseongdae, Gyerim Forest, Gyochon Village and Woljeonggyo Bridge, and ends with Donggung Palace and Wolji Pond lit up at dusk.

As of November 17, 2026 — opening hours can change. Check once more before you go.

The walking alone adds up to under an hour, but with sightseeing and breaks it takes half a day to a full day. Start in the afternoon and time it so you reach Donggung Palace and Wolji Pond around sunset, and you'll see both the daytime views and the night lights.

1. Start at Hwangnidan-gil

Hwangnidan-gil (황리단길) is the street running along Poseok-ro on the west side of Daereungwon. Old houses and hanok built in the 1960s and 70s have been turned into cafes, restaurants, gift shops, photo studios and hanok stays.

The name comes from Gyeongnidan-gil, a famous street in Itaewon, Seoul. People started calling it "the Gyeongnidan-gil of Hwangnam-dong" about ten years ago.

What sets it apart from busy streets in Seoul or Busan is the height. Tall buildings aren't allowed here to protect the heritage sites, so low tiled roofs run along the street, with 1,500-year-old Silla tombs rising behind them. Seeing royal tombs from a cafe window is something you won't find in any other city.

It is especially popular with young travelers. It was named Destination of the Year at the 2025 Korea Tourism Stars awards, and foreign visitors have grown sharply since Gyeongju hosted the APEC summit in 2025. From January to September 2026, more than 1.16 million foreign visitors came to Gyeongju, up 19.6% from a year earlier.

Why start here?

  • It's a short walk from where you arrive: About 10 minutes on foot from the Gyeongju bus terminal, and most city buses from Gyeongju Station (KTX) also stop nearby.
  • Eat before you set off: Once you start walking, long stretches pass through parks and heritage sites. It's easier to have lunch on Hwangnidan-gil first.
  • The Daereungwon back gate is right there: At the end of the street, walk in through the Daereungwon back gate and the route carries straight on.

For a snack, try Hwangnam-ppang, Gyeongju's signature red bean pastry. It got even more famous when Korea's president gave it to China's president as a gift during the APEC summit. You'll also find sibwon-ppang, a bread shaped like a 10-won coin, all along the street.

2. Daereungwon: a park of Silla royal tombs

Daereungwon (대릉원) is a park with 23 large tombs believed to belong to Silla kings, queens and nobles. The big ones are 47 to 82 meters across, so they look like small hills.

The largest, Hwangnamdaechong, is over 22 meters tall. Walking between tombs as high as a 7- or 8-story building, you get a real sense of how the people of Silla honored their kings.

Go in through the back gate on the Hwangnidan-gil side, walk between the tombs and leave by the main gate, and you come out right by Cheomseongdae.

Don't miss the inside of Cheonmachong. In 1973, before digging the largest tomb, Hwangnamdaechong, archaeologists excavated this one first as practice. Yet this "practice" tomb yielded 11,526 artifacts, including a gold crown and a gold belt.

One of them was a painting of a flying horse on birch bark, the Cheonmado (Heavenly Horse painting). It was the first painting ever found in a Silla tomb, so it became a National Treasure, and the tomb was named after it.

Cheonmachong is believed to date from the late 5th to early 6th century. So the gold crown and the Cheonmado found here were used by the people of Silla about 1,500 years ago.

Inside the tomb, you can see the wooden chamber and stacked-stone structure as they were. The real artifacts are at the Gyeongju National Museum, introduced in section 6, so it's more fun to see both.

The spot where a single magnolia tree stands between the tombs is Daereungwon's most famous photo spot. The stone-wall path from the back gate to the main gate is also a lovely walk.

3. Cheomseongdae: Silla's observatory

Cheomseongdae (첨성대) is an observatory built during the reign of Queen Seondeok of Silla (632–647), and is considered the oldest observatory in East Asia. It became a National Treasure in 1962.

At about 9 meters tall, it's smaller than you might expect. But the interpretations that give meaning to each stone are fun.

  • 27 layers: The round body is built of 27 layers of stone. One reading links this to Queen Seondeok being the 27th ruler of Silla.
  • About 362 stones: One reading says this stands for the number of days in a lunar year.
  • Middle window: There's a square window about halfway up. People likely climbed a ladder to the window, then climbed inside to the top to observe the sky.

In fact, opinions are still divided over whether Cheomseongdae was really an observatory or a place for rituals. It's a riddle with no answer, which makes you look at it a little longer.

About 7 minutes on foot from the Daereungwon main gate, it stands in the middle of a wide lawn. At night it's lit up, and it looks quite different from the daytime.

4. Gyerim: the forest of a Silla king's birth legend

Gyerim (계림) is an old forest 3 minutes on foot from Cheomseongdae. Ancient willows and zelkova trees grow so thick they cover the sky.

This forest holds the birth story of Kim Al-ji, the founder of Silla's royal Kim clan.

One day a rooster was heard crowing in the forest. People went to look and found a golden box hanging from a branch, with a white rooster crowing beneath it. When they opened the box, a baby boy came out, and because he came from a golden box, he was given the surname Kim (金, meaning gold).

The forest, once called Sirim, became Gyerim, "the forest of the rooster" (gye, 鷄). Gyerim was even used for a time as a name for Silla itself. A pavilion in the forest shelters a stone monument recording Kim Al-ji's birth, set up in 1803.

The shade is deep, so it's a good place to rest on a sunny day. The path through the forest leads on to Gyochon Village.

5. Gyochon Village and Woljeonggyo Bridge: a hanok village and a wooden bridge

Gyochon Village (교촌마을) stands on the site of Gukhak, Silla's first national academy, founded in 682. The school carried on as Hyanghak (a local school) in the Goryeo period and Hyanggyo (a local Confucian school) in the Joseon period, so the village became Gyochon, "the village with the Hyanggyo".

The most famous house in the village belongs to the Choe family, known as the rich Choe family of Gyeongju. For about 300 years from the mid-1600s, the family kept a large fortune from generation to generation. They moved to Gyochon in 1779, and the old house still standing in the village is where they lived from then on.

They kept a family rule: never take a government post above jinsa (진사), the basic degree from the state civil service exam.

Korea was under Japanese colonial rule from 1910 to 1945. Koreans call this period the Japanese occupation (일제강점기).

The last of the rich Choes, Choe Jun, ran a Busan trading company called Baeksan Sanghoe during the Japanese occupation and sent the company's money to the Provisional Government of the Republic of Korea, set up in Shanghai, China, by independence activists, to fund the independence movement. After liberation, he gave his remaining fortune to education.

The family's story even appears in Korean elementary school textbooks. You can still see the old house inside the village.

Woljeonggyo Bridge, over the Namcheon stream in front of the village, was first built in 760 under King Gyeongdeok of Silla. It was used for over 500 years before it disappeared. Excavation began in 1984, and the restoration was finished in 2018, 34 years later.

At 66 meters long, it's a roofed pavilion bridge and the largest wooden bridge in Korea. You can walk across it.

6. Gyeongju National Museum: the real artifacts from Daereungwon

The Gyeongju National Museum is close to the road from Woljeonggyo to Donggung Palace and Wolji Pond. The real artifacts from Daereungwon and Donggung Palace and Wolji Pond are gathered here, making it like the answer key to this walking route.

In 2025, during the APEC summit, the museum hosted Korea–US and Korea–China summit meetings. That same year, a special exhibition brought all six Silla gold crowns together for the first time, and annual visitors topped 1.97 million, the most in 30 years.

Silla gold crowns: why are they special?

Six Silla gold crowns have been found so far. The first was discovered in 1921, and all of them came from Silla tombs in downtown Gyeongju. The Cheonmachong and Hwangnamdaechong crowns came from Daereungwon.

  • The meaning in the shape: The branch-shaped ornaments at the front are said to stand for a sacred tree linking heaven and earth, and the antler-shaped ones at the back for abundance and heavenly power. The design shows that the king's power came from heaven.
  • A symbol of power: In Silla, only the king, the royal family and the highest nobles could own gold ornaments. A gold crown was something only the very highest could wear.
  • Craftsmanship: Small gold ornaments and green curved jade pieces were hung one by one on the gold sheets with gold wire. The Cheonmachong crown is so finely made that it carries engraved lines only about 0.25 mm thick.

The director of the Gyeongju National Museum has called the Silla gold crowns the most original and accomplished masterpieces among ancient East Asian ornaments.

The Divine Bell of King Seongdeok: a sound from 1,250 years ago

This bell, hanging in the museum grounds, is 3.66 meters tall and weighs 18.9 tons. King Gyeongdeok of Silla began making it to honor his father, King Seongdeok, and his son, King Hyegong, finished it in 771. The work took 34 years.

  • A sound that fades and swells: When the bell is struck, the sound keeps fading and swelling as it rings on. This is called maeknori (beating), and it comes from the unique shape and materials of Korean bells. The Divine Bell of King Seongdeok's sound swells about every 3 seconds.
  • The resonance pit: Right under the bell is a hollow dug into the ground. It is said to gather the sound and make it resonate.
  • Carved images and text: The body is carved with two pairs of flying celestial beings and 1,037 characters of text. The text says that when the bell was finished, "its shape stood tall like a mountain, and its sound was like a dragon's cry."

It also has a nickname, the Emille Bell. It comes from a sad legend that a child was offered when the bell was cast, so the bell's ring sounds like a child crying "emille" for its mother. It is only a legend.

Wolji Hall: Silla raised from a pond

This gallery holds artifacts found in the pond at Donggung Palace and Wolji Pond, introduced in section 7. Along with a wooden boat, 10 panbul, flat gilt-bronze Buddha plaques, were found there. Seeing them before you head to the pond in the evening makes the night view feel different.

7. Donggung Palace and Wolji Pond: time it for sunset

Donggung Palace and Wolji Pond is the site of a pond made in 674 and a detached palace built in 679 by King Munmu of Silla, who unified the Three Kingdoms. It was where the kingdom welcomed guests and held banquets.

After Silla fell it lay in ruins, and in the Joseon period it was called Anapji, "the pond where geese and ducks fly in." Excavations revealed the original name, and it was renamed in 2011.

When the pond bed was examined from 1975, over 33,000 artifacts came out. Among them was the Juryeonggu, a 14-sided die carved with forfeits like "Empty your cup and laugh out loud" and "Dance without a sound." So people in Silla were playing drinking games 1,300 years ago.

The pond was built with curving edges so you can never see all of it from one spot, and the view changes as you walk.

When the lights come on at night, the three restored halls are reflected perfectly in the pond. It's Gyeongju's most famous night view.

If you go in a little before sunset, you can see the sunset and the moment the lights come on. Sunset times change with the seasons, so check that day's sunset time and arrive about 30 minutes before.

A full loop around the pond takes about an hour. Last entry is 9:30 p.m., so don't leave it too late.

8. Getting back

You can walk back from Donggung Palace and Wolji Pond to Hwangnidan-gil, but after a full day on your feet, a bus or taxi is easier.

  • Woljeong circular bus: Passes Donggung Palace and Wolji Pond and returns to Hwangnidan-gil Public Parking Lot. It stops early in the evening, so check the last bus time.
  • City bus: Buses 10 and 11 pass nearby and go to the bus terminal.
  • Taxi: Catch one in front of Donggung Palace and Wolji Pond, or call one with a taxi app.

Bus routes are covered in the city bus section of Busan to Gyeongju by train or bus.

9. Check before you go

  • Shoes: You'll be walking all day, so wear comfortable shoes.
  • Timing: The museum closes at 6 p.m., and Donggung Palace and Wolji Pond are best at sunset. Decide first whether to include the museum.
  • Cheonmachong: Daereungwon is free, but going inside Cheonmachong needs a separate ticket.
  • Last entry: Daereungwon and Donggung Palace and Wolji Pond stop letting people in at 9:30 p.m.
