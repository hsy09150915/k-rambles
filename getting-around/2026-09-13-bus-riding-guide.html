How to Ride the Bus in Korea — From Front/Back Doors to Center-Lane Stops

Not sure which door to use, or whether you're waiting for the right bus going the right way? Here's the "why" behind it and exactly how to check before you board.

As of September 14, 2026 — stop layouts and routes can vary by city and section.

A center bus-only lane — the stop platform sits right on the dedicated lane, separated from regular traffic.

Riding a bus in Korea for the first time can feel a little nerve-wracking. Which door do you get on through? Is this stop even going the direction you want? And why is there a stop sitting in the middle of the road out of nowhere? These are things travelers genuinely get tripped up on, so let's walk through each one with the reasoning behind it.

If you remember nothing else
  • Check the stop location and direction on a map app before you go — it's the most reliable way to be sure.
  • A number followed by "분" (minutes) on the arrival board is the time left until that bus arrives.
  • "OO행"/"OO방면" on a bus is often a major stop partway along the route, not the final destination.
  • Board through the front door when you can — if the bus is too crowded to reach the front, boarding through the back door is fine too.
  • Korea drives on the right, so bus doors are on the right side — and stops are often on a center island in the middle of the road (a center lane).

Read "OO분" on the Arrival Board

Most stops have an electronic board showing bus arrival times. The number next to the route and the Korean character "분" (minutes) are what matter — for example, "3분" means that bus arrives in 3 minutes. "곧 도착" means it's arriving any second, within about a minute.

The number + "분" next to the route number is the time left until arrival; the name on the far right is the final destination.

Remember the Shapes "OO행" and "OO방면"

The text on the front or side of a bus reading "OO행" or "OO방면" names the area that bus is headed toward. Even without knowing the exact meaning, just recognizing the shape of "행" or "방면" is enough to spot at a glance: "ah, that's this bus's direction."

Buses work a little differently from the subway here, though. On the subway, "OO행" is usually the name of the line's actual last stop. On a bus, "OO행"/"OO방면" is often a major stop or area partway along the route, not the true final destination. If you don't see your destination's name, that doesn't automatically mean it's the wrong bus — it's worth double-checking on the arrival board's destination column or a map app to see whether that bus actually passes your destination.

Either way, the shapes "행" and "방면" show up the same way on the subway too, so learning to recognize them pays off well beyond just buses.

Board Through the Front Door, Exit Through the Back

Korean city buses generally work on a front-door-boarding, back-door-exit system.

  • Boarding — through the front door, tapping your card as you get on
  • Exiting — through the back door, tapping your card as you get off

It's a little different when the bus is packed, though. If the area by the front door is too crowded to squeeze through, locals often just board through the back door too. There's a card reader back there as well, so tapping your card there works exactly the same for payment. And if people are already getting off, boarding through the back door once they've cleared out is completely fine.

When in doubt

When the bus isn't crowded, boarding through the front door is the default courtesy. Think of back-door boarding as something you use only when the front is genuinely too packed to reach.

Korea Drives on the Right

Korean roads run on a right-hand traffic system. Cars travel on the right side of the road, and bus doors are mounted on the right side of the bus as well. That's why, when a bus pulls up to a stop, its right-side door always faces the platform. Once you have this basic principle down, the center-lane stops covered next make a lot more sense.

So Stops End Up in the Middle of the Road — Center Lanes

Walk down a major street in Seoul or Busan and you'll notice some bus stops aren't along the curb at all — they're on an island in the middle of the road that you reach by crossing a crosswalk. This is a center bus-only lane (중앙차로).

On these stretches, there's no stop on the sidewalk side. Which means if you just board whatever bus you see nearby without checking, you can easily end up heading the wrong direction — this is something travelers genuinely mix up often.

Bus-only lanes originally ran along the curb like regular traffic, but they kept getting blocked by cars turning right or parking illegally. So the lane was moved to the middle of the road entirely for buses, with island-style stops built alongside it. Seoul first introduced this on Cheonho-daero in 1996, and it's expanded steadily since.

The takeaway

Heading the opposite direction almost always means crossing the road first. The surest habit is checking, with the map app covered above, whether the stop you're standing at is actually the one for your direction.
