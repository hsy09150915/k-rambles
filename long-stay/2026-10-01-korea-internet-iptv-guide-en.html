Korea Home Internet & IPTV Setup, Fully Explained for Foreigners

Which plan to pick, the 3 telecoms' real contact info, the sign-up process, how billing works, and alternatives beyond the big 3 — everything you need to actually get installed over the phone.

As of October 2026

1. Which plan should I sign up for?

When you call a telecom, they'll ask right away: "Internet only, or bundled with TV? Do you want a landline too?" If you haven't decided beforehand, it's easy to end up signing up for things you don't need just from listening to the rep's pitch. Pick based on your own usage pattern from the table below first.

For reference, you don't need to source a Wi-Fi router separately. SK Broadband and LG U+ include a router free with every plan, and KT includes it free only on plans of 1Gbps or above (plans under 1Gbps, like 100Mbps or 500Mbps, may add a KRW 1,100/month fee or require a separate purchase) — it's worth confirming "is the router included?" once when you sign up.

Bottom line

If you're mostly on Netflix and YouTube, "internet-only 500Mbps + a smart TV" is the cleanest, cheapest combination.

For reference, Netflix's official recommended speed for 4K is about 15Mbps, so 100Mbps looks like enough on paper — but once you factor in the speed loss through a Wi-Fi router and multiple devices at once, 500Mbps feels considerably more stable in practice.

If you really need to watch Korean TV broadcasts, you can add IPTV at that point — there's no need to bundle the triple play (internet + TV + phone) from the start.

2. The 3 telecoms — where and how to sign up

There are actually far more internet providers in Korea than just KT, SK Broadband and LG U+ — including regional cable operators. But realistically, for a foreigner facing a language barrier, these are the only 3 you can actually rely on (plus the "budget internet" resellers on these 3 networks covered in section 5 below). The general customer service lines (100, 106, 101, etc.) are all Korean-only by default, so we've left those out and only listed the channels that actually work in a foreign language.

The document needed across the board is your Alien Registration Card (ARC) (SK Broadband also accepts a Domestic Residence Report Card (국내거소신고증, for overseas Koreans), a diplomat ID, or a USFK ID). If someone applies on your behalf, they also need your passport, a signed consent form from you, and the proxy's own ID in addition to the registration card.

Not sure how long you'll stay? — KT Prepaid Internet

If a long-term stay isn't a sure thing yet, KT's Prepaid Internet is worth a look. There's no 1- or 2-year contract — you pay by credit card for just the period you need, in 3-month, 6-month or 12-month units (choose by speed: Essence (1Gbps), Basic (500Mbps), or Slim (100Mbps); installation fee and equipment deposit are separate; no refund on early termination).

You can use it with no early-termination penalty to worry about, and just let it end when the term is up.

The site states the consultation line (080-448-0100) supports English, Chinese, Vietnamese and Russian, but as with KT's general consultation line above, an actual call may only offer English and Chinese — if you specifically need Vietnamese or Russian, it's safer to double-check before calling.

3. Sign-up process — finishing it in one phone call

  1. 1

    Decide on a plan, then call

    Contact the foreign-language-capable number (or callback request page) of the telecom you chose above directly. There are price/freebie comparison sites like Danawa and Tongsinnara, but they're all Korean-only, so they're hard to use if you need a foreign language — it's most reliable to just call directly with the plan you already decided on in section 1.

  2. 2

    Check installation availability

    When you give your address during the phone consultation, they'll check availability right away. Officetels and newer villa buildings sometimes only support certain telecoms. If you're in a villa, check with your landlord or building manager first about whether a line is already installed — internet might already be included in your rent.

  3. 3

    Apply + submit documents

    Whether by phone or in-store, your Alien Registration Card is the key document. SK Broadband often also asks for a copy of a bankbook tied to your Alien Registration Card (accounts opened with a passport are often not accepted, so check beforehand).

  4. 4

    Book an installation date

    You usually book your preferred date within a few days to a week of applying. The exact lead time varies by region and scheduling, so it's best to confirm directly during the consultation.

  5. 5

    Installation day

    A technician visits to install the router (Wi-Fi) and do a simple identity check. Someone must be home at the scheduled time.

  6. 6

    Activation complete

    Once installation is done, you can use the internet right away. If you bundled IPTV, ask the technician on the spot for the basic remote control operations.

4. How does billing work

  • You can use either automatic transfer from a Korean bank account or automatic card payment — register either right at sign-up, or later through the telecom's app or customer service.
  • However, both require an account or card issued in Korea. Visa/Mastercard cards issued abroad often can't be registered for Korea's recurring-payment (auto-debit) system even if the brand matches — it's safer to open a Korean bank account with your Alien Registration Card first, then use a domestic debit/credit card issued on that account.
  • If you don't set up auto-pay, you'll get a paper bill (called a "giro" — a printed slip you can pay in cash at a bank or convenience store) at home each month, or a bill via text/app notification, and you have to pay it manually each time.
  • Like building maintenance fees, this is a "falls behind if you don't pay it separately" type of charge, so for long-stay residents it's recommended to set up auto-pay right when you first sign up.

5. Are there alternatives to the big 3 telecoms?

Budget internet (resold internet) — these products run entirely on the big 3's own networks but charge a cheaper monthly rate.

Line quality (speed, stability) is identical to the big 3's direct service, but at the same speed the price tends to be about 25–35% lower than the direct rate — for example, KT Skylife's 3-year-contract rates are KRW 16,500 for 100Mbps / KRW 22,000 for 500Mbps / KRW 25,300 for 1Gbps (VAT included, confirmed October 2026).

For reference, KT Skylife's official FAQ states that foreigners can also sign up directly on the website with just an Alien Registration Card (ARC) (self-activation requires identity verification via Naver, Kakao, a credit card, or PASS).

In short, budget internet has a clear price advantage, but don't expect foreign-language support. If Korean-language consultation feels like too much, it's more reliable to look into the foreign-language channels of the big 3 above first (especially KT, which has the strongest multilingual support, then SK Broadband's foreigner-only plan) — but if you can handle it in Korean yourself, or have someone around to help, budget internet comes out considerably ahead on price. It's also worth preparing a translation app (camera translation) before calling, or using an interpretation service like BBB Korea (1588-5644) alongside it.

Apartment elevator noticeboards / local agents for the big 3 — in large apartment complexes or officetels, it's common to see regional cable operators' internet ads posted on the elevator noticeboard.

These regional cable operator plans are often just as cheap as budget internet, and signing up through a local agent for the big 3 often comes out cheaper than the price listed on their websites.

The catch is that these channels are mostly Korean-only, with no separate foreign-language support. So if you have a Korean acquaintance who can help, signing up through such a local agent or noticeboard listing — under their name or through their referral — is economically much better: the line ultimately runs on the same big-3 networks (or networks that lease from them), so there's essentially no quality difference, just a difference in price.

LTE/5G wireless routers (internet with no installation) — just plug it in and it works, with no technician visit needed.

This can be a good alternative if your stay is only a few months, or if waiting around for an installation technician feels like too much hassle.

That said, the data allowance is limited (roughly 11GB-60GB a month) and the speed-for-price is lower than wired internet, so for a long stay, wired internet ends up more economical.

Pocket Wi-Fi rental — a short-term product for travelers, billed by the day.

It's inefficient for a long stay of several months or more, so just keep it in mind as a reference option.

This article is for general informational purposes — rates, freebies and promotions vary by telecom and change frequently depending on timing and the specific agent. Before actually signing up, be sure to confirm the latest terms directly by phone or in-store.
